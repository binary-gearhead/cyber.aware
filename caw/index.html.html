02 // COMMON VECTORS
Where ordinary people actually get hit
Two channels account for most everyday incidents: the networks you borrow, and the platforms you already trust.

Public Wi-Fi

  • Evil twin hotspots — a network named almost exactly like the real one, built to catch anyone who isn't looking closely
  • Man-in-the-middle — an attacker sitting between you and the sites you visit once you share their network
  • Fake captive portals — a "sign in with Google/Facebook" login screen that exists only to harvest your credentials
  • Session hijacking — stealing the token that keeps you logged in, without ever needing your password
HTTPS has closed most of the old "read your password over the air" attacks. What's left are the active tricks above — fake networks and fake logins, not passive snooping.

Social Media

  • Fake support DMs — urgent warnings that your account will be locked, linking to a convincing clone of the real login page
  • Credential stuffing — passwords leaked elsewhere, tried against your accounts until one fits
  • Impersonation — cloned or hijacked profiles pushing investment scams and "giveaways"
  • Oversharing — pet names, schools, and travel plans quietly answer your own security questions for an attacker
  • AI-assisted scams — cloned voices and deepfaked video making the con far more convincing than a typo-ridden email ever was
Account takeover is now one of the most common forms of identity crime the general public faces — and it rarely needs sophisticated tools, just your trust.
03 // ATTACK SURFACE
The habits that quietly let attackers in
Almost anything that connects to the internet can be turned against you if it's left on its defaults.
// 01

Juice jacking

A tampered public USB port can move data or malware the moment you plug in — it looks completely ordinary. Carry your own charger, or use a wall socket instead.

// 02

Bluetooth left on

Idle Bluetooth and "share with everyone" AirDrop settings open the door to tracking and unsolicited access. Switch both off when you're not actively using them.

// 03

Smart home / IoT

Cameras, plugs, and speakers often ship with weak default passwords and rarely get updated — a single compromised device can open your whole home network.

// 04

Digital exhaust

Boarding passes, house numbers, and casual check-ins build a profile attackers can use for password resets and eerily personalised phishing.

// 05

Phishing, everywhere

SMS, WhatsApp, email, or a phone call — the human element is present in the large majority of successful breaches. One OTP given away is often all it takes.

// 06

Urgency as a weapon

"Act now or lose access" is the single most reliable trick in the playbook. Genuine institutions rarely rush you — treat urgency itself as a red flag.

04 // FIELD REPORTS
What "high" and "critical" look like
Not everyday risks — but the reason the same basic hygiene matters at every scale, from your phone to a national grid.
2021
Colonial Pipeline

Entry point: a single compromised VPN account with no multi-factor authentication. What happened: ransomware spread through IT systems, and out of caution the operator shut down a pipeline carrying roughly 45% of fuel to the U.S. East Coast. Fallout: fuel shortages, panic buying, and a $4.4 million ransom — a fair share of which was later recovered. The industrial control systems were never even touched; the disruption came entirely from the IT side.

2020
SolarWinds

Entry point: malicious code slipped into a trusted software update used by thousands of organisations, including U.S. government agencies. What happened: installing the update quietly opened a backdoor into those networks. Fallout: widely attributed to a state-linked actor, and considered one of the most significant supply-chain compromises on record — proof that even well-defended organisations can be reached through a vendor they trust.

05 // SELF-DIAGNOSTIC
Has your data already leaked?
A free, well-established tool called Have I Been Pwned lets anyone check whether an email address has surfaced in a known data breach.

Type an email address in, and it's checked against a huge archive of confirmed breaches. If your address turns up, you'll see exactly which services leaked it and roughly what kind of data was exposed — passwords, phone numbers, sometimes more. If it doesn't, you get a clean bill of health for that address.

It's not a hacking tool and it doesn't store what you search in any way that's tied back to you for lookups. It's simply a mirror — worth checking every few months, especially for the email you use for banking.

06 // LEGAL FRAMEWORK
Digital crime, physical penalties
A screen doesn't make an offence less real. These are the laws that protect you as a victim — and apply just as much if you're ever the one crossing the line.

INDIA — IT ACT, 2000 (AS AMENDED)

SEC. 43 / 66

Unauthorised access or damage to computer systems. Civil compensation and criminal penalties.

SEC. 66C

Identity theft — misusing someone's password or digital signature. Up to 3 years plus fine.

SEC. 66D

Cheating by personation using a computer resource — classic online fraud. Up to 3 years plus fine.

SEC. 66E

Violating privacy by capturing or publishing private images without consent.

SEC. 67 / 67A / 67B

Publishing obscene, sexually explicit, or child sexual abuse material — the most severely punished offences in the Act.

SEC. 66F

Cyber terrorism — attacks aimed at India's unity, security, or sovereignty. Among the harshest sentences on the books.

SEC. 75

Extends the Act beyond India's borders when a crime touches a computer system located in India.

INDIA — DPDP ACT, 2023 & GLOBAL FRAMEWORKS

DPDP ACT

Gives you rights to access, correct, and erase your personal data, and requires organisations to have a lawful basis to process it. Penalties for misuse can run into hundreds of crores. Full enforcement is being phased in toward 2027.

GDPR (EU / UK)

Strong data rights — access, erasure, portability — for anyone whose data is handled by a company serving the EU/UK, regardless of where that company is based.

CFAA (USA)

The main U.S. anti-hacking statute, criminalising unauthorised access to protected computers. Penalties range from misdemeanours to years in prison depending on intent and harm.

BUDAPEST CONVENTION

The most widely adopted cybercrime treaty, ratified by 80+ countries, standardising how illegal access, fraud, and CSAM offences are prosecuted and investigated across borders. India has not signed on, but cooperates through its own laws.

The same laws that protect you as a victim apply to your own conduct too — attempting to "hack back," scrape data without permission, or share intimate images without consent carries real, prosecutable consequences.
07 // INCIDENT RESPONSE
If something's already gone wrong
Speed matters far more than perfection. Report early — funds can sometimes still be frozen in the first golden hours.
1930

FILE A COMPLAINT ONLINE
cybercrime.gov.in →
Your local police cyber cell and the Indian Cyber Crime Coordination Centre (I4C) also handle these cases directly.
Freeze first. Call 1930 immediately for financial fraud — quick action can still stop a transfer in progress.
Lock down accounts. Change passwords and enable app-based two-factor authentication on anything that might be exposed.
Save the evidence. Screenshot messages, links, and transaction IDs before anything can be deleted or edited.
File officially. Report through cybercrime.gov.in or your local cyber cell — verbal complaints alone rarely trigger action.
Alert your bank. Notify them directly for card or account-linked fraud, in parallel with the police report.
Warn your circle. If a social account was hijacked, tell your contacts before the attacker tries to scam them too.
08 // DAILY PROTOCOL
The habits that cover most of it
You don't need expensive tools. Consistency with a handful of habits handles the majority of what actually reaches ordinary people.
Use a password manager, or at least unique passwords everywhere
Turn on app-based two-factor authentication for email, banking, social
Prefer mobile data over public Wi-Fi for anything sensitive
Use a reputable VPN when public Wi-Fi is the only option
Avoid public USB charging ports — bring your own charger
Keep Bluetooth and AirDrop off unless actively in use
Change default passwords on every smart home device
Never share an OTP with anyone who calls or messages you
Set social profiles to private and trim your friends list
Install OS and app updates as soon as they're offered
Keep backups of important files, both cloud and offline
Know your local helpline before you ever need it
